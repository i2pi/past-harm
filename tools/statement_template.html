PAST
HARM

Joshua Reich · Generative Works 1996–2026 · technical statement

Three programs, written years apart, run as one piece. past (December 2003) is a generative music engine: sixteen oscillators, a few scales and a nest of slow sine waves. harm (2003, rendered 2006) is a set of feedback delay networks with drums. p10m1 (March 2012) is a feedback picture of rotating cubes. Here past plays, harm listens to it, and the picture reads past's internal state. This page walks through the ideas and the mathematics, starting with the music, with the code quoted directly from the source. The 2026 version was made with Claude, and each section says which parts are original and which were added (section 16).

1Signal flow

directedCombining past, harm and p10m1 into one piece, with the picture reading the music's state, was Joshua's idea. ClaudeThe wiring is Claude's: the struct port of past, audio output, snapshots for the picture, and the offline renderer.

All the sound comes from one function, past_next(), called 44,100 times a second. Its sample index i is the only clock: every slow change in the piece is a function of i, so the piece is deterministic. Rendered twice, it is identical to the sample. The picture runs at its own frame rate and reads snapshots of the engine's state: which voices sound, their notes and envelopes, the key, the drum hits, the echo gains.

2How a voice is made

originalpast, December 2003, kept verbatim. The code below is Joshua's. past alone still renders byte-identical to the 2003 program.

past has sixteen oscillators. A note sets an oscillator's phase increment from its frequency and gives it an attack / hold / release envelope. What a voice sounds like depends on its slot j: it sums N = 2 + (j mod 7) partials, weighted linearly down from the fundamental:

wk = (N − k) / (N(N−1)/2),   k = 0 … N−1    (Σwk = 1)

Even partials are plain sines at k times the phase. Odd partials go a quarter as fast, at k/4 times the phase, through an arctangent waveshaper whose drive is itself a sine of period 2π·319732 samples, about 45 s:

odd k:   wk · atan( sin(kφ/4) · 7 sin(i/319732) )

When the drive is near zero the partial is a soft sine; at full drive the arctangent squares it off and fills it with odd harmonics. The drive passes through zero twice a cycle, so every voice slowly breathes between pure and buzzing. Odd and even slots are routed to different accumulators (x, y and u, v), which become left and right.

3The cross-coupled filter

originalpast, 2003, verbatim. ClaudeThe picture reading these coefficients is a 2026 mapping.

Before the output, the two channels are fed back into each other through the previous three samples. The coefficients are sines with long, unrelated periods, about 10 s, 30 s, 34 s and 2 min, some offset by the current tick. Together they form a time-varying second-order recursive filter in which left drives right and right drives left:

{{xfb}}

Because the periods share no common factor, the filter never repeats its settings. The colour and stereo image of the whole texture drift endlessly, even while the notes repeat. The output then goes through atan(·)/1.6, a soft clipper, so the feedback can never run away. The picture uses these same coefficients: the background's radial warp, the blur and the feedback decay all read them.

4Scales, keys and harmony

originalThe scale tables are past's. directedMaking full passages more harmonious, and a sparse, oppressive opening, were Joshua's requests. ClaudeThe method is Claude's: key finding by Krumhansl–Kessler correlation, snapping to the scale, pentatonic once four voices sound, and the Phrygian and in scales for the opening. In the full piece this code moves some of past's notes: the engine is unmodified, but in dense passages the pitches heard are snapped to a scale past did not choose.

past's melody comes from short scale tables: offsets in semitones such as {0, 3, 0, 5, 0, 1, 7, 4, 5}, stepped through by a counter. Notes can collide: with up to sixteen voices and three tables, a full passage can stack semitones and tritones. Three additions keep the harmony consonant without changing past's choices.

Finding the key

Every 512 samples a 12-bin pitch-class histogram is built from the scale tables and the sounding voices, weighted by their envelopes. It is correlated (Pearson's r) against the Krumhansl–Kessler probe-tone profiles, rotated to all 12 tonics in major and minor; the best of the 24 is the key. A change of key has to win for about 4 s, or by a clear margin, so the key doesn't flicker.

r(h, p) = Σ(hi−h̄)(pi−p̄) / √( Σ(hi−h̄)² · Σ(pi−p̄)² )
{{key}}

Snapping to the key

Each new note moves to the nearest pitch in a mask of the key's 12 pitch classes, rounding ties down. Which mask depends on how full the music is. With fewer than four voices, the full seven-note scale is allowed. With four or more, only the pentatonic, the five notes with no semitone or tritone between any two of them, so a dense chord cannot clash. During the opening, while the mood is dark, the masks are Phrygian, minor with a flat second, and then the Japanese in scale (root, ♭2, 4, 5, ♭6), which is darker still.

5The arc and the mood

directedStarting sparse, building up and down, and an oppressive opening that opens out were Joshua's. ClaudeThe curves (150 s × 330 s, the 0:20–3:20 smoothstep), the octave drop, the lament and the darkening low-pass are Claude's.

How many voices may sound at once follows a raised cosine of period 150 s. Its height is modulated by a second raised cosine of period 330 s, so no two swells are the same size. The cap runs from 2 to 16 voices, so the piece opens sparse, builds, and thins out again, and the drums, the choir and the bell come in as it rises.

w(t) = ½(1 − cos 2πt/150) · (0.8 + 0.2·½(1 − cos 2πt/330)),    cap = 2 + round(14w)
{{cap}}

Over that, the mood rises once, as a smoothstep from 0 to 1 between 0:20 and 3:20. While it is low, the notes drop an octave, the harmony uses the dark masks, a single choir voice sings a lament around the root, and the whole mix passes through a low-pass filter that starts at 900 Hz and opens (cutoff 900·24.5·mood Hz). The piece begins sparse and oppressive and opens out.

6The choir: PADsynth

directedThe choir, a single solo voice at the start, and the technique (Gaussian bands in the frequency domain, random phases, a saw's overtone series) were Joshua's. ClaudeThe implementation, the bandwidths (45 and 12 cents), the tables per octave and the vowel formants are Claude's.

past's long notes, the bass pairs, are sung by a choir. A choir is many voices that are almost, but not quite, in tune. Rather than detune a few oscillators, the choir is built in the frequency domain, after Nasca Octavian Paul's PADsynth. For a base pitch f0, each harmonic h becomes a Gaussian band whose width grows with h, at saw-wave amplitudes 1/h:

|X(f)| = Σh (1/h) · exp(−((f − hf0)/bh)²) / √bh,    bh = (2c/1200 − 1) · f0 · h

Each frequency bin then gets a random phase, and one inverse FFT of 218 points turns the whole spectrum into a 5.9-second loop. Because every bin has its own random phase, the loop sounds like many singers each slightly off pitch, and it is perfectly seamless. The ensemble is 45 cents wide; the solo voice of the opening is 12 cents wide (narrower, so it sounds like one person). One table is built for each octave of base pitch, then read at the note's pitch through three vowel formants that drift between "ah" and "oh".

7The bass

directedA heavy, solid bass, then thicker, an octave lower, then leaner, were Joshua's calls. ClaudeThe design (pure fundamental, detuned voices, tanh, low-pass) and its constants are Claude's.

Under the choir sits a sub-bass that follows its lowest note, folded by octaves into 20–50 Hz. It is a pure fundamental, for weight, plus three voices detuned by −6, 0 and +6 cents. Each detuned voice carries its 2nd and 3rd harmonics, and because they beat slowly against each other the body moves. It all goes through a tanh saturator and a low-pass filter. Its weight, the level, the amount of harmonic body, the drive and the cutoff, rides one of the long swells (section 12).

8Coprime polyrhythms

directedMeaningful polyrhythms in past, harm and the choir, drums that guide the listener toward them, and using harm's own drum sounds, were Joshua's. originalThe thump, tick, blip and sweep are harm's 2003 clicks, re-timed. ClaudeThe divisions (3, 3+3+3+3+4, 5, 7, 11:8), pairing each stream with a drum that plays at full then drops to 40%, and the drum timbres (log drum, rim, FM bell, tick) are Claude's.

past ticks on a grid of 8192 samples, 0.186 s. Sixteen ticks make a bar of 131,072 samples, 2.97 s, and every rhythm in the piece is a division of that bar. Two pulses dividing the same bar into a and b beats coincide at fractions m/a = n/b, that is, every 1/gcd(a, b) of a bar. If a and b are coprime (gcd = 1), they meet only on the downbeat. Between downbeats every onset of one falls in a different place against the other, and the gaps between successive onsets of the combined pattern are all different. That is what lets the ear hear two streams instead of one.

onsets of a ∩ onsets of b = { k / gcd(a,b) }  ⇒  gcd = 1: only the downbeat

The piece runs five streams at once. Each musical stream, a voice that is part of the music itself, is paired with a guide drum that plays its pulse. When a stream is new to the ear, its drum plays at full level for four bars, then drops to 40% as a light doubling, and the music carries the rhythm on its own:

past's accents3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 4past's own short notes are accented in groups of 3 of its 16 steps: 3 against 4 inside its own gridrim

9Rhythm that mutates

directedThe design is Joshua's: new coprime seeds, delay times that change, mutations staggered, one random change about every 7 minutes. ClaudeThe value pools, the coprimality rule, the guide drum's return and the 1% tape-style glide are Claude's.

In endless mode the five divisions are genes. About every seven minutes (randomly 6–8, always on a downbeat), one gene is picked at random and given a new value from its pool. The new value must be coprime with the other beats (the accent grouping is exempt), so the streams can never lock together. The changed stream's guide drum comes back to full for four bars, pointing the ear at the new rhythm.

{{mutate}}

When a delay grid mutates, harm's taps do not jump to their new lengths, which would click. They glide at most 0.01 samples per sample, reading between samples with linear interpolation:

y = d[⌊τ⌋] + (τ − ⌊τ⌋)(d[⌊τ⌋+1] − d[⌊τ⌋]),    |dτ/dt| ≤ 0.01  ⇒  pitch ratio 1 ± 0.01 (≈ 17 cents)

A delay line whose length changes shifts the pitch of what passes through it (the tape-delay effect): its echoes bend by at most 17 cents for the 10–40 s that the change takes, then settle on the new grid.

10harm's delay networks

originalharm, 2003: convolve(), the networks and their moving gains, verbatim. ClaudePutting the taps on fractions of past's bar, the stability scaling and the de-clicking glides are Claude's.

harm is a bank of seven feedback delay networks. Each is a single circular buffer, 50,000 samples long, read at up to six taps. The taps feed forward to the output with gains gf and back into the buffer with gains gb. In z-transform terms each network is an all-pole comb filter with a direct path:

s[n] = x[n] + Σk gbk s[n − τk],    y[n] = g0x[n] + Σk (gfk + gbk) s[n − τk],    H(z) ∝ 1 / (1 − Σ gbk z−τk)

{{convolve}}

harm's gains are themselves slow sines, so the echoes swell and fade. Its feedback could run away: by the triangle inequality the network is stable when Σ|gbk| < 1, and some of harm's tap sets reach 1.77. Each set is therefore scaled so that its sum is at most 0.95. The other change is that the taps no longer sit at harm's arbitrary lengths. They sit on fractions of past's bar, so the echoes are themselves rhythms, in 5 on the left and 7 on the right. A tap longer than the buffer folds down an octave.

11A reverb that fills the gaps

directedA reverb that swells to fill in where the voices are sparse was Joshua's. ClaudeThe FDN design and its mapping are Claude's.

The reverb is an eight-line feedback delay network (FDN). The lines are mutually prime lengths, 2011 to 4507 samples, so their echoes never pile up on the same sample. They are mixed through an orthogonal Hadamard matrix (energy-preserving, every line feeding every other) behind four all-pass diffusers per side. Each line's gain is set so that the tail decays by 60 dB in RT60 seconds:

gi = 10−3 Li / (RT60 · fs),    H8 = H2 ⊗ H2 ⊗ H2,   H2 = [[1, 1], [1, −1]] / √2
{{fdn}}

What makes this reverb particular is what drives it. It follows how full past is: the sum of the sixteen voice envelopes, smoothed, rising fast (0.8 s) and falling slowly (2.5 s). When past thins out, the reverb swells: the wet level rises from 0.06 to 0.70 and the tail lengthens from 2.2 s to 7.5 s. The space fills in exactly where the notes leave it.

12Long swells and endlessness

directedRunning the mathematics on endlessly, and dialling the choir and bass up and down on coprime 5- and 9-minute sines, were Joshua's. ClaudeWhat each swell changes (ranks, width, drive, cutoff) is Claude's.

past was written to last about eight minutes; its sections are counted in minutes and seconds. In endless mode the section clock wraps every 8 minutes, the tick period has a floor, and notes are folded back into range, so the mathematics runs on indefinitely. Above that sit slow controls whose periods are chosen to be coprime, so their combinations take a long time to repeat:

13The picture

originalp10m1, 2012: the feedback, blur and matte look, the cube layout and the palette. Claude ported them to the GPU, keeping the integer arithmetic. directedMore cubes on different axes tied to the music, inverse zoom (5× at no voices), and an orange lattice through the black that is driven by the score and comes back changed, were Joshua's. ClaudeThe circle-of-fifths mapping to axes, and the lattice's specific variants and colours, are Claude's.

p10m1 is a feedback image. Every frame, the previous frame is blurred, and the newly rendered cubes replace it only where they are, with the old picture showing through their insides. The blur and the feedback emulate the original's integer arithmetic exactly, truncating toward zero, so small values always lose a little and the interiors sink toward black over many frames. That slow sinking is p10m1's look.

{{feedback}}

Cubes that orbit with the notes

180 cubes are divided among the sixteen voices; each cube's size follows its voice's envelope. Each voice's group of cubes turns about an axis taken from the note it plays. The pitch class is placed on the circle of fifths (multiply by 7 mod 12), so related notes turn on nearby axes, and the octave tilts the axis. A semitone step reverses the direction. The orientation is a quaternion integrated every frame, so a change of note is a slowing down and turning back, never a jump.

θ = 2π · (7·pc mod 12)/12,   φ = 22° + 16°·octave,   axis = (sin φ cos θ, cos φ, sin φ sin θ),   q ← Δq(axis, ω·dt) · q
{{orbit}}

The lattice in the black

Where the feedback has sunk to near black, an orange triangular lattice shows through. Its four layers pulse with the four polyrhythm layers, and its spacing, thickness, hue and repetition follow the score. When it fades out it comes back changed: rotated, sheared by an affine map, warped, less dense, and recoloured within an orange / teal / blue family. Repetition, then surprise.

Zoom

The camera zooms in inversely to the number of voices sounding: about 5× when past is silent, 1× at ten or more voices. As the music thins out, the picture moves closer.

14Glass

directedGlass cubes that refract and disperse, lossy and imperfect (bumps, fractures), and the corrections to them, were Joshua's. ClaudeThe physics and the shaders (tangent-space flaws, Snell refraction, absorption, Fresnel, dispersion) and their constants are Claude's.

About one cube in nine is glass. A separate pass renders the glass cubes' surface normals into a half-float buffer. Some glass is perfect, some gently bumped, some strongly bumped, some fractured into shards. The flaws are a tiling height field stored as slopes along the face's own two edge directions (tangent space). A small shader tilts each face's normal by the slope along its actual edges, so a bump bends light the way the face is really turned, whatever the cube's rotation:

n′ = normalize(n − (∂h/∂u) t − (∂h/∂v) b)
{{flaw}}

The glass pass bends the view ray at that normal by Snell's law (refractive index 1.5) and looks up the picture where the bent ray lands. Each colour channel bends by a slightly different amount, which gives dispersion, the rainbow fringes. Light is absorbed along the path through the glass (Beer–Lambert), more in red than in blue, and grazing faces reflect more (Fresnel):

η1 sin θ1 = η2 sin θ2,    T = exp(−σ · path),   σ = (0.17, 0.12, 0.08) for r, g, b
{{snell}}

15A title that gets eaten

directedThe title, its text, D-DIN, and its being eaten by the picture were Joshua's. ClaudeThe integrity mechanism is Claude's.

The title, PAST / HARM over the credit, is set in D-DIN with CoreText into a coverage mask in the bottom left. Each text pixel has an integrity that can only fall. Wherever the evolving picture differs from the plain background behind the letters, they are nibbled away, raggedly, and the damage creeps inward from the bitten edges. The title is stamped in before the feedback, so its fragments are dragged into the blur and the echoes. Within a couple of minutes, the work has eaten its own name.

16Who made what

This version was made in November 2026 by Joshua Reich working with Claude, Anthropic's AI model. He set the direction and the constraints, chose the techniques in many places, and judged every result. Claude wrote most of the new code and made many of the detailed choices. Counting code lines only (comments and lone braces excluded, renamed variables matched), about 260 of the roughly 2,800 lines are carried over verbatim from the original programs:

Line counts alone mislead. Most of the piece's character comes from the original systems: past's composition and synthesis, harm's networks and p10m1's feedback picture. Every 2026 addition started from Joshua's request, and those requests often named the technique (Gaussian bands with random phases, coprime seeds, 5- and 9-minute sines, the lattice in the black, the eaten title). The Claudelabels mark where the specific choices were Claude's, among them the drum sounds, the harmony method, the reverb, the glass physics and many constants. Those are a large part of what is heard and seen, and they are credited here as such.
